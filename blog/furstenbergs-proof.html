Infinity in a Finite Space

Furstenberg's Topological Proof of the Infinitude of Primes

Topology Number Theory Interactive

We all know there are infinitely many prime numbers. Euclid proved this over 2,000 years ago with a beautiful, simple argument. But in 1955, a mathematician named Harry Furstenberg offered a completely different proof—one that uses topology, the study of space and shapes.

What makes this proof famous (and slightly notorious) is that it uses the language of "open sets" and "closed sets" to prove a fact about numbers. It feels like using a sledgehammer to crack a nut, but the sledgehammer is made of pure crystal and the nut reveals a universe inside.

Don't worry if you've never heard of topology. We're going to build the intuition from scratch, visually.

The "Shape" of Integers

In topology, we define the "shape" of a space by deciding which sets of points are "open". For Furstenberg's proof, we define an Arithmetic Progression as our building block.

An arithmetic progression is just a sequence like "all even numbers" ($2n$) or "all numbers ending in 1" ($10n + 1$).

The Proof Logic

Here is the step-by-step logic, translated from "Topology" to plain English:

  1. The Building Blocks: We declare that any arithmetic progression (like "multiples of 3") is an "Open Set".
  2. The Union: If you combine any number of Open Sets, the result is still Open.
  3. The Complement: The "Complement" of an Open Set is a "Closed Set".
    Crucial Point: The set of multiples of a prime $p$ is Closed. Why? Because the "rest" of the numbers (those not divisible by $p$) can be formed by combining other arithmetic progressions.
    (Example: If we take multiples of 3, the "rest" are numbers $3n+1$ and $3n+2$, which are also arithmetic progressions!)

The Contradiction

Now, assume for a moment that there are only finitely many prime numbers.

If the list of primes were finite (say, just 2, 3, and 5), then the union of all their multiples would be a finite union of closed sets.

In topology, a finite union of closed sets is always Closed.

So, the set of ALL multiples of ALL primes would be a Closed set.

What is left over? The only numbers that are not multiples of any prime are 1 and -1.

Since the "Multiples" set is Closed, its opposite (the remaining part, $\{-1, 1\}$) must be Open.

But here is the catch: In this specific topology, any non-empty Open set must be infinite. You cannot have a finite Open set. It's impossible to fit an infinite arithmetic progression inside the tiny set $\{-1, 1\}$.

Contradiction! We concluded that $\{-1, 1\}$ is Open, but we know it cannot be Open because it is finite. Therefore, our initial assumption—that there are finitely many primes—must be false.

Conclusion

Furstenberg's proof connects the discrete world of integers with the continuous concepts of space. It shows that the infinitude of primes is not just a property of multiplication, but a structural necessity of the number line itself.
