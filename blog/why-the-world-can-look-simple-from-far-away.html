Why the World Can Look Simple From Far Away

A beginner's guide to scaling laws, universality, and why a neat pattern does not necessarily predict what happens next.

Complexity Scaling Laws Beginner Friendly

Imagine looking down at a city from an airplane. You see roads, neighborhoods, and streams of traffic. The whole city seems to have a shape. Now land, stand at one junction, and try to predict exactly which driver will change lanes in the next ten seconds. Suddenly, the simple picture disappears.

That contrast is the subject of a fascinating survey I recently read. It travels through biology, financial markets, artificial intelligence, prime numbers, physics, and computer science. At first, these topics seem to have almost nothing in common. Yet each contains a similar tension: the big picture can follow a compact rule while the small details remain stubbornly difficult.

The central idea: A crowd may be predictable even when the people inside it are not. A simple rule for the average does not automatically give us a shortcut to every individual outcome.

The survey calls this macro-compressibility with micro-irreducibility. That is a formidable phrase, but the intuition is friendly. “Macro” means the large-scale view. “Compressible” means we can summarize it with a short rule. “Micro” means the individual details. “Irreducible” means there may be no known shortcut for producing those details one by one.

Up close: the micro view

One organism, one trade, one AI response, one prime gap. Many details matter, and exact prediction is difficult.

→
Zoomed out: the macro view

Average metabolism, typical market impact, model loss, prime density. A compact pattern can emerge.

First, what is a scaling law?

A scaling law describes how one quantity changes when another quantity becomes larger. Suppose we compare animals of different sizes. As body mass increases, how does energy use increase? Or suppose we train larger AI models. As we add data and computing power, how does their average error change?

A common mathematical form is a power law:

$$Y \approx A x^\alpha$$

Here, $x$ is the thing we change, $Y$ is the result we measure, $A$ sets the overall scale, and the exponent $\alpha$ tells us how quickly the result grows or shrinks.

If $\alpha=1$, doubling $x$ doubles $Y$. If $\alpha=2$, doubling $x$ makes $Y$ four times as large. If $\alpha=1/2$, doubling $x$ increases $Y$ by only about 41 percent. The useful feature is that one exponent can summarize behavior across many sizes.

Power laws are appealing because they turn a cloud of observations into a short sentence. But they are also easy to oversell. A roughly straight line on a log–log chart may cover only a limited range. The slope can change when the data, definitions, or measurement method changes. A good fit is evidence for a pattern; it is not automatically a law of nature.

Scaling is not the same as universality

In everyday speech, “universal” means something happens everywhere. In physics, the word has a stricter meaning. Near certain phase transitions—such as the point where a magnet loses its magnetism—very different materials can display the same large-scale behavior.

Physicists explain this using the renormalization group. Imagine repeatedly blurring a high-resolution image. Tiny scratches and individual pixels disappear. What remains are large shapes. Near a critical point, repeated “blurring” of a physical system can make many microscopic details irrelevant. Systems with the same important features, such as dimension and symmetry, can then approach the same large-scale pattern.

This is genuine universality because it comes with more than a similar-looking exponent. There is a defined way to zoom out, a limiting regime, and a mechanism explaining why the discarded details stop mattering. Outside this setting, it is safer to say empirical scaling regularity unless an equally strong explanation exists.

One article, four levels of confidence

The most helpful habit in the survey is that it does not put every claim on the same shelf. It separates results into four levels:

Established

A proved theorem or a widely replicated measurement with a clearly stated range.

Empirical regularity

A repeated pattern in data that still depends on the model and measurement choices.

Conditional or conjectural

A conclusion that depends on an assumption or a limiting claim that is not yet proved.

Hypothesis

A proposed way to organize ideas that must still earn support through tests.

This distinction matters because a theorem about prime density is not the same kind of knowledge as a curve fitted to financial data. Both can be useful. They simply justify different levels of confidence.

Example 1: animals do not obey one magic exponent

Biology

Larger animals generally use more energy than smaller animals, but energy use does not rise in direct proportion to body mass. This relationship is called metabolic allometry. It is often written as $B=aM^b$, where $B$ is metabolic rate and $M$ is body mass.

A famous idea known as Kleiber's law places the exponent $b$ near three quarters. An influential model linked this pattern to the branching networks that deliver resources around an animal's body. The result is elegant, memorable, and genuinely useful.

But “three quarters for every living thing” is too strong. Research finds that the exponent varies with species group, temperature, age, activity, and physiological state. The right lesson is not that scaling failed. It is that a compact relationship can describe a regime without being a universal constant.

Body mass may help us estimate the average energy demand of a kind of animal. It will not tell us the exact metabolic activity of one particular animal at 3:17 p.m. The macro pattern survives; the micro event needs more information.

Example 2: markets have patterns, not crystal balls

Finance

Financial markets produce several recurring statistical patterns. Large price movements occur more often than a simple bell curve would predict. Volatile days tend to arrive in clusters. Buy and sell orders can remain correlated over long periods.

One especially interesting regularity concerns market impact: how much a large sequence of trades tends to move the price. Across many datasets, average impact is often close to the square root of the order's size relative to normal market volume. In plain language, making an order four times as large tends to produce roughly twice the average impact, after suitable normalization.

This does not mean every order follows the curve. Researchers must decide where one large order begins and ends, how to measure normal volume, and how to adjust for volatility and market conditions. Change those choices and the fitted curve can change.

Most importantly, an average impact curve is not a machine for predicting the next price move. It summarizes many trades under specified conditions. Knowing the shape of traffic at rush hour does not tell us what one driver will do at one junction.

Example 3: bigger AI models improve along a curve

Artificial intelligence

Large language models also show scaling relationships. Over a controlled range, their average prediction error—usually measured as a loss—often falls smoothly as researchers increase model size, training data, and computing power.

These curves are valuable engineering tools. They can help a team estimate how much improvement to expect from a larger training run and how to balance the amount of data against the number of model parameters. This is macro-compressibility in action: a complicated training process is summarized by a few resource variables.

However, the curve depends on the architecture, data mixture, optimizer, training setup, and the definition of compute. It describes average performance within a regime. It does not tell us what internal idea a model has learned, whether a rare capability will appear, or exactly how the model will answer your next prompt.

So AI scaling laws are closer to carefully calibrated engineering curves than to a proven universality class in physics. They are reproducible and useful without being magic.

Example 4: we know how dense the primes are, but not the next gap

Number theory

Prime numbers are integers divisible only by 1 and themselves: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, and so on. They are completely deterministic. Yet their local spacing looks irregular enough that probability is often useful for describing it.

The prime number theorem is an established result. It says that the number of primes up to a large number $x$ is approximately $x/\log x$. From far away, this gives us an excellent map of prime density. It also tells us that the average gap between nearby primes around $x$ is roughly $\log x$.

But the theorem does not tell us the exact size of the next gap. Probabilistic models suggest useful distributions for ordinary and extreme gaps, yet some of the strongest versions depend on unproved conjectures. The primes are not secretly random; rather, random models can capture selected statistics while arithmetic constraints still matter.

There is an even stranger connection. The spacings between zeros of the Riemann zeta function show remarkable statistical agreement with eigenvalues of certain random matrices. Numerical evidence is strong, and parts of the theory are established, but the complete connection and the Riemann hypothesis remain unproved. This is a perfect case for careful labels: striking evidence is not the same as a completed theorem.

“Hard to predict” does not automatically mean P versus NP

This is where several attractive ideas are often mixed together. P versus NP is a precise question about decision problems. Roughly speaking, P contains problems that a computer can solve efficiently as the input grows. NP contains problems where, if the answer is “yes,” a proposed solution can be checked efficiently. Whether every efficiently checkable problem is also efficiently solvable remains unknown.

Computational irreducibility is broader and less formal. It suggests that for some processes, the fastest way to learn a future state may be to run the process step by step. This is an important idea, but it is not another name for P not equaling NP.

Likewise, random constraint problems can show phase transitions. As a random 3-SAT problem gains more constraints, it moves from usually solvable to usually impossible, and instances near the boundary can be difficult for algorithms. That resemblance to a physical phase transition is useful. It still does not settle a worst-case statement such as P versus NP. In fact, random 2-SAT has a sharp threshold even though 2-SAT belongs to P.

The same warning applies to primes. A density formula not revealing the next prime does not make primality testing NP-complete. We have a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm for testing whether a number is prime. “My coarse formula cannot predict the next event” and “no efficient algorithm can solve this formal problem” are entirely different claims.

The useful synthesis: simple above, difficult below

We can now return to the survey's big proposal. A system is macro-compressible when a compact model predicts a large-scale quantity over a clearly stated range. It might predict average metabolism, average market impact, language-model loss, or prime density.

The same system may be micro-irreducible relative to a specific task if we know no comparably efficient shortcut for obtaining an exact trajectory, answer, or next event. The phrase “relative to a specific task” is essential. Without naming the task, the input, and what counts as efficient, irreducibility is only a metaphor.

The proposal is not that biology, markets, AI, and primes share one hidden equation. It is a way to ask better questions across fields. What are we averaging? What survives when we zoom out? What exact outcome are we trying to compute? How strong is the evidence for each claim?

A short summary: Description and computation are different jobs. A small equation may describe a distribution very well while offering no shortcut to a particular member of that distribution.

How to read the next “universal law” headline

When a chart or headline claims that the same law appears everywhere, I now want to ask four questions:

  1. What exactly is being predicted? An average, a distribution, an individual event, or a worst-case computation?
  2. Over what range? A relationship that works across ten observations may fail outside those observations.
  3. What changes the result? Definitions, normalization, sampling, temperature, architecture, or market conditions may alter the exponent.
  4. What kind of evidence is it? A theorem, a replicated empirical fit, a result conditional on a conjecture, or a new hypothesis?

A fifth question is useful when someone uses the word “universal”: is there a mechanism showing why microscopic differences disappear, or do two lines merely look similar? Similarity can be a productive clue. It should not borrow the proof status of a different field.

What I took away

The world can be regular without being simple all the way down. Physics gives the strongest example: at a critical point, a genuine theory explains why different microscopic systems share large-scale behavior. Biology, markets, and AI offer useful but conditional scaling patterns. Number theory combines rigorous global laws with conjectural local models. Computer science reminds us to define “difficulty” before making claims about it.

That is a more modest conclusion than “everything follows one universal mathematics,” but I find it more interesting. It tells us why prediction can succeed and fail at the same time. We can understand the shape of a forest without knowing the position of every future leaf. We can compress the crowd while each individual story remains open.
